# The Swiss Mandatory Health Insurance System: OKP/KVG Features and the Risikoausgleich

## 1. Historical and Legal Background

Switzerland's mandatory health insurance system — the *Obligatorische Krankenpflegeversicherung* (OKP), governed by the *Krankenversicherungsgesetz* (KVG) — was enacted on 18 March 1994 and entered into force on 1 January 1996. It replaced the *Bundesgesetz über die Krankenversicherung* (KUVG) of 1911, which had operated for eighty-five years as a partially voluntary, heavily fragmented system of sickness funds with no universal enrollment obligation.

The KVG reform was driven by three concurrent pressures. First, the KUVG system had produced chronically incomplete coverage: in 1994, approximately 3–4% of the Swiss population remained uninsured, and coverage conditions varied substantially across the roughly 1,100 Krankenkassen (health insurance funds) then in operation. Second, healthcare expenditure had been growing faster than GDP throughout the 1980s, partly as a consequence of fragmented cost-sharing arrangements and partly due to the combination of aging and medical technology diffusion. Third, the political economy of the reform reflected a desire to institutionalize competition among insurers — following the logic of managed competition developed by Enthoven (1988) and adapted for European social insurance systems — while preserving the solidarity principles that characterized the Swiss welfare state.

The KVG rests on three foundational principles that remain in force today. *Universality*: every resident in Switzerland must hold a basic health insurance policy with an approved Krankenkasse; failure to enroll within three months of establishing residence triggers automatic enrollment by the cantonal authority. *Solidarity*: premiums are set on a community-rated basis — they cannot be differentiated by the health status, medical history, or expected costs of individual enrollees, only by age band, residence, and choice of deductible level. *Competition*: the provision of OKP is reserved for private-law entities (Krankenkassen and commercial insurers holding a special authorization from the Federal Office of Public Health, BAG), which compete for enrollees, negotiate with service providers, and must operate their OKP business as a separate accounting unit from supplementary insurance.

## 2. Universal Enrollment and Open Enrollment

The obligation to enroll is imposed on every person resident in Switzerland, including foreign nationals and cross-border workers who meet the Swiss residency criteria under Article 3 of the KVG. Exemptions exist for diplomats, members of certain religious communities, and military personnel covered under separate schemes, but these are quantitatively negligible. Cantons maintain lists of approved Krankenkassen offering coverage in their territory and are empowered to enforce enrollment by selecting and contracting on behalf of uninsured residents.

From the insurer's side, Article 4 KVG imposes an *obligation to insure* (*Kontrahierungszwang*): a Krankenkasse that is active in a given canton must accept any applicant for OKP who resides in that canton, regardless of age, health status, or pre-existing conditions. The insurer cannot refuse enrollment, impose waiting periods, or charge loading factors for high-risk individuals under the OKP mandate. This creates the fundamental tension that motivates the Risikoausgleich: insurers cannot price risk in premiums, but they face the same underlying heterogeneity in medical costs.

Enrollment flexibility is maintained through an annual open enrollment window. Enrollees may switch Krankenkassen at the end of each calendar year, with a three-month notice period (i.e., notice by 30 November for a change effective 1 January). The flexibility to switch, combined with community rating, creates a strong incentive for healthy individuals to hold low-premium policies and for sick individuals to seek the insurer offering the most comprehensive service network — a canonical adverse selection dynamic that the Risikoausgleich is designed to mitigate. In practice, switching rates are modest (approximately 5–8% of adult enrollees annually) but concentrated among healthy young adults, reinforcing risk segregation across the insurer landscape.

## 3. Community Rating and the Premium Structure

Under the KVG, Krankenkassen set premiums at the level of the *Prämienregion* (premium region) rather than at the individual level. Switzerland is divided into three premium regions per canton (urban, peri-urban, and rural), yielding a national total of roughly 90 distinct premium regions. Within a premium region, the Krankenkasse charges the same monthly premium to all adult enrollees in the standard-deductible (Franchise) plan.

Three age bands are distinguished:
- **Children (0–17 years)**: premia are substantially lower, with the difference partly absorbed by the Prämienverbilligung subsidy system.
- **Young adults (18–25 years)**: premia are approximately 50% of the adult rate, a reduction introduced to improve affordability for young enrollees.
- **Adults (26 years and older)**: the standard community-rated premium, which varies by insurer and by premium region.

Premiums are submitted annually to the BAG for approval. The BAG reviews whether the proposed premium covers the expected claims costs plus a permissible administrative loading and reserve contribution. Effective from 2022, cantonal governments also have a formal say in premium approval for their canton, introducing an additional political constraint on premium growth. The BAG does not impose a binding premium cap, but it can reject submissions that it deems unjustifiably high relative to projected claims costs.

Within the adult group, enrollees may choose among several deductible (Franchise) options: CHF 300, 500, 1,000, 1,500, 2,000, or 2,500 per year. Higher franchise levels attract proportionally lower premiums. This creates within-insurer segmentation: healthy individuals self-select into high-deductible plans (paying lower premiums and bearing more first-dollar risk), while chronically ill individuals cluster in the standard low-deductible plan. The Franchise differentiation therefore acts as a partial second dimension of premium variation that is correlated with health risk — a notable imperfection in the community rating constraint.

## 4. Standardized Benefit Package and Cost-Sharing

The OKP benefit package is defined exhaustively in federal law (KVG and the implementing Ordinance, KVV) and covers acute medical treatment, preventive services, hospitalization, pharmacy, certain dental care, physiotherapy, and medical devices. The benefit package is identical across all Krankenkassen for OKP coverage — insurers may not offer enriched or restricted benefit sets within the OKP mandate. This standardization is essential to the functioning of the risk equalization system, because it ensures that cost differences between insurers reflect differences in risk pools rather than differences in coverage generosity.

Co-payments take two forms. First, the annual franchise (*Jahresfranchise*) is a deductible borne entirely by the enrollee before insurance coverage activates; the enrollee pays the full cost of all services up to the franchise threshold. Second, conditional on having exceeded the franchise, the enrollee bears a 10% co-insurance rate (*Selbstbehalt*) on all costs, up to an annual maximum of CHF 700 for adults and CHF 350 for children. This cost-sharing structure, while partially standard across OECD systems, has been criticized for generating access barriers for low-income individuals with high medical needs, which is the primary justification for the Prämienverbilligung subsidy program (discussed in Section 6).

Alternative managed-care plan models — *HMO* (health maintenance organization) and *Hausarztmodell* (general-practitioner-centered care) — are permitted within the OKP framework and typically offer premium reductions of 10–20% in exchange for constraints on direct access to specialists or hospital care. These models are not a departure from community rating within the model type: all enrollees in the same model, age band, and region pay the same premium. They do, however, introduce a further dimension of risk segmentation, since healthier individuals are more willing to accept gatekeeping constraints.

## 5. Supplementary Insurance and Its Relationship to OKP

The KVG explicitly separates mandatory basic insurance (OKP) from voluntary supplementary insurance (*Zusatzversicherung*), which is governed by private law under the *Versicherungsvertragsgesetz* (VVG). Supplementary products include:

- **Enhanced hospital coverage**: semi-private or private ward entitlement, free choice of physician, coverage in Swiss hospitals outside the enrollee's canton.
- **Dental coverage**: not included in the OKP benefit package except for accident-related treatment.
- **Alternative medicine**: homeopathy, acupuncture, and other complementary treatments not fully covered by OKP.
- **Foreign coverage**: enhanced coverage for healthcare incurred abroad.

Unlike OKP, supplementary products are risk-rated: Krankenkassen may charge individually differentiated premiums, apply waiting periods, or exclude pre-existing conditions. Most supplementary products require a medical questionnaire and can be declined for high-risk applicants. This creates a two-tier market: the OKP tier, where solidarity is enforced through community rating and open enrollment, and the VVG tier, where actuarially fair pricing is permitted. Insurers are legally required to maintain strict accounting separation between OKP and supplementary products, and cross-subsidization in either direction is prohibited.

## 6. Premium Subsidies (Prämienverbilligung)

Community rating, by construction, charges the same absolute premium to a healthy 35-year-old earning CHF 200,000 per year and a chronically ill 55-year-old earning CHF 30,000 per year. For low- and middle-income households, OKP premiums consume a disproportionate share of disposable income: by 2023, the average OKP premium in Switzerland had reached approximately CHF 450–550 per adult per month, representing 6–10% of median disposable income.

To address this regressivity, the KVG mandates a system of premium subsidies (*Individuelle Prämienverbilligung*, IPV) funded jointly by the federal government and cantons. Under Article 65 KVG, cantons are required to reduce premiums for households whose insurance burden exceeds a canton-specific income threshold. The federal government contributes a lump-sum transfer to cantons indexed to the national average OKP cost, which cantons supplement with their own funds according to cantonal policy.

The generosity and structure of the IPV system varies substantially across cantons. As of 2022, the federal IPV contribution amounts to approximately CHF 5.7 billion annually, with total federal and cantonal spending approaching CHF 9–10 billion — roughly 30% of total OKP premium revenue. Recipients are concentrated in the lower three income quintiles. Despite its scale, the IPV has been criticized for incomplete targeting: many eligible households in middle-income cantons receive modest subsidies, while a significant share of expenditure reaches households that are not at significant affordability risk. Federal legislation enacted in 2022 (in force from 2024) introduced a binding floor: cantons must now ensure that no household spends more than 10% of its net income on OKP premiums, substantially strengthening the targeting obligation.

## 7. Insurer Market Structure and Regulation

As of 2024, approximately 50 Krankenkassen hold OKP authorization in Switzerland, down from roughly 1,100 at the time of the KVG's introduction. This consolidation reflects both market dynamics — economies of scale in administrative processing and risk pooling — and deliberate regulatory decisions. The insurer market is highly concentrated in several cantons, with the five largest groups (CSS, Helsana, Concordia, Visana, Swica) collectively holding approximately 75% of enrolled lives.

Regulatory oversight is divided between two federal bodies:
- The **Bundesamt für Gesundheit (BAG)** oversees compliance with the KVG mandate: premium approval, benefit coverage, enrollment obligations, and financial reserves.
- The **Eidgenössische Finanzmarktaufsicht (FINMA)** supervises the financial soundness of insurers, including the solvency requirement for the OKP book. OKP and supplementary insurance face different reserve requirements and solvency standards.

The reserve requirements for OKP are risk-weighted: each insurer must maintain reserves at a level calibrated to the volatility of its claims history and the size of its enrolled population. The Risikoausgleich transfers, by reducing the effective cost volatility across insurers, mechanically reduce the minimum reserve requirement for high-risk pools — a direct solvency benefit that is discussed formally in the economic model.

Industry coordination occurs through **Santésuisse**, the umbrella association of Swiss health insurers, which negotiates national tariff agreements (notably the *SwissDRG* system for inpatient care and *TARMED* for outpatient care) on behalf of the insurer community. These tariff agreements set the price schedule for medical services independent of the Risikoausgleich, which operates on the revenue side.

## 8. Risikoausgleich: Mechanism and Evolution

The Risikoausgleich (RA) is the transfer mechanism that redistributes premium revenues across Krankenkassen based on the expected cost profile of their enrolled populations. Without the RA, community rating would create an extreme incentive to enroll only low-risk individuals: an insurer with a younger, healthier pool than the system average would earn consistent profits at the system-average premium, while an insurer with an older, sicker pool would face systematic losses. The RA eliminates — or at least reduces — this incentive by compensating high-risk pools with net transfers funded by levies on low-risk pools.

### 8.1 Phase 1 (1996–2011): Age and Sex Cells

The initial RA formula, in force from 1996, calculated expected costs for each insurer's pool based solely on the age and sex composition of enrolled individuals. Switzerland is divided into age-sex risk cells: age is grouped in five-year intervals from 0–4 to 90+, giving approximately 20 age groups; combined with sex (two categories), this yields roughly 40 risk cells. For each cell, the average national cost is computed annually from the claims data submitted by Krankenkassen to the BAG. The expected cost for insurer j is then:

> Ĉ_j (Phase 1) = Σ_{a,s} N_{j,a,s} × c̄_{a,s}

where N_{j,a,s} is the number of enrollees in insurer j belonging to age-sex cell (a, s), and c̄_{a,s} is the national average cost for that cell. The RA transfer is:

> T_j = Ĉ_j − N_j × c̄

where c̄ is the system-wide per-capita average cost. Insurers with pools that are more expensive than average on the age-sex dimension receive positive transfers; those with cheaper-than-average pools make contributions.

The Phase 1 formula was administratively simple and transparent but was widely recognized as insufficient. Age and sex explain roughly 10–15% of the variation in individual healthcare expenditures. The majority of cost variation is driven by morbidity — the presence of chronic conditions, multimorbidity, and disability — which is not captured by demographic proxies. As a result, Phase 1 left substantial incentives for risk selection on health status dimensions, particularly since the formula ignored all information about diagnoses, treatments, or pharmaceutical utilization.

### 8.2 Phase 2 (2012–2019): Hospital and Nursing Home Stays

The 2012 reform added a binary indicator of prior-year hospital or nursing home admission to the RA formula. Individuals who had spent at least one night in a hospital or nursing home in the prior calendar year were classified as "high-risk" within their age-sex cell, and separate cost averages were computed for this sub-group. This doubled the number of risk cells from approximately 40 to approximately 80, and significantly improved the predictive accuracy of the formula.

The addition of the hospitalization indicator was a deliberate second-best choice. Diagnostic information (such as ICD-10 codes) would have been more informative but raised concerns about data infrastructure readiness, coding incentive effects, and administrative burden on smaller Krankenkassen. The hospital stay flag was available from existing claims data, required no new diagnostic coding infrastructure, and was found in pre-reform analyses to explain a significant share of cost variation beyond age and sex.

Empirical analyses of the Phase 2 period suggest that the expansion raised the R² of the RA prediction model from approximately 10–15% to approximately 20–25%, reducing cross-insurer loss ratio variance by a meaningful but not dramatic amount. Cherry-picking continued to be documented — particularly through plan benefit design and network restrictions — but its profitability was reduced.

### 8.3 Phase 3 (2020–): Pharmaceutical Cost Groups (PCG)

The most recent and most significant reform added *Pharmazeutische Kostengruppen* (PCG — Pharmaceutical Cost Groups) to the RA formula, effective from 2020. The PCG system is based on an individual's active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) consumption in the prior year. A list of 22 PCG groups has been defined, each corresponding to a class of medications associated with a specific chronic or high-cost condition: diabetes (insulin, oral antidiabetics), cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease (COPD, asthma), cancer, HIV/AIDS, immunosuppressants (post-transplant), and others. An individual is assigned to a PCG group if they received at least two prescriptions for drugs in that therapeutic class in the prior 12 months.

The Phase 3 formula retains the age-sex cells and hospital stay indicators from Phase 2 but adds 22 binary PCG indicators, substantially expanding the risk cell structure. Empirical estimates suggest that the Phase 3 formula raises the R² of cost prediction to approximately 25–35%, reflecting the substantially higher cost levels associated with chronic disease groups. Individuals in the highest-cost PCG groups (transplant recipients, cancer patients on biologics) have expected costs that are 3–5 times the population average; capturing this variation in the RA formula substantially reduces the incentive for selective deterrence of these groups.

The PCG reform also introduced a concern about formula gaming: an insurer theoretically benefits from under-prescribing drugs in the PCG classes, since this would reclassify individuals as lower-risk under the RA formula while maintaining the same actual claims cost exposure. To date, there is no robust empirical evidence of such manipulation in Switzerland, but it represents a recognized regulatory risk and motivates the ongoing discussion about transitioning to a diagnosis-based RA system.

The transfer formula under Phase 3 is:

> Ĉ_j (Phase 3) = Σ_{a,s,h,p} N_{j,a,s,h,p} × c̄_{a,s,h,p}

where h ∈ {0,1} is the hospital stay indicator and p ∈ {0,...,22} indexes PCG group membership (0 indicating no PCG assignment). The transfer T_j remains defined as Ĉ_j − N_j × c̄.

## 9. Ongoing Policy Debates

### 9.1 Adequacy of the PCG Formula

The PCG formula represents a significant advance over the age-sex-hospital formula, but it remains imperfect by construction. Several limitations are recognized in Swiss policy discussions. First, the PCG groups cover a relatively narrow set of chronic conditions and miss many high-cost patient groups — particularly those with rare diseases, severe mental illness, or complex multimorbidity that does not map neatly onto a single drug class. Second, the PCG indicator uses pharmaceutical consumption as a proxy for morbidity, which introduces a systematic bias against individuals with equal underlying morbidity but lower pharmaceutical adherence (e.g., patients who decline medication for chronic conditions). Third, the one-year lookback window means that newly diagnosed patients or those who recently started treatment are not yet captured in the PCG, creating a lag in adjustment that temporarily over-compensates or under-compensates insurers in transition years.

Proposals under active political discussion include extending the lookback window to three years, adding diagnostic cost groups based on inpatient ICD coding, and introducing episode-based cost groupers similar to those used in the Netherlands' ACG-based system.

### 9.2 Premium Growth and Affordability

Swiss OKP premiums have grown substantially faster than wages over the past two decades. Between 2002 and 2024, average adult premiums more than doubled in nominal terms, reaching approximately CHF 520–560 per month in the lowest-premium region and over CHF 650 in Geneva. This growth reflects genuine cost-side pressures (technology, aging, pharmaceutical innovation) but also structural inefficiencies in the tariff setting system, particularly the outpatient TARMED tariff which has not been revised since 2004 and has been widely criticized for generating volume incentives. The federal government and Santésuisse are currently engaged in negotiations to replace TARMED with a new tariff structure (TARDOC), with implementation targeted for 2026.

### 9.3 Consolidation and Market Power

The decline from ~1,100 to ~50 Krankenkassen since 1996 raises concerns about insurer market power, particularly in cantonal markets where two or three funds may hold the majority of enrolled lives. Concentrated markets reduce the competitive pressure on premium efficiency and may weaken the disciplining function of open enrollment. Against this, consolidation may improve risk pooling stability and administrative efficiency. The BAG has not yet established a formal concentration threshold for regulatory intervention in the insurer market.

### 9.4 Cantonal Variation in Subsidies

The decentralized structure of the IPV creates horizontal inequities: residents of identical income and health status face substantially different net premium burdens depending on their canton of residence. Cantons with generous subsidy schemes (Basel-Stadt, Zurich, some Romand cantons) provide substantially higher per-capita IPV than fiscally constrained cantons. The 2024 federal reform establishing the 10% income cap is expected to reduce this variation, but implementation will depend on cantonal fiscal capacity and political will.
